# Emerging Topic Discovery

## Purpose

`emerging-topic-discovery` identifies research areas that are genuinely beginning to develop rather than merely becoming popular.

Its central question is:

> Which scientific topics, concepts, methods, technologies, or research combinations are beginning to form a meaningful new research frontier?

An emerging topic may involve:

* a newly appearing concept;
* a new scientific question;
* a rapidly developing method;
* a new technology applied to an established problem;
* a new mechanism;
* convergence between previously separate disciplines;
* a new data source;
* a newly testable hypothesis;
* transition into a new stage of evidence maturity.

Emergence must be supported by multiple signals.

---

# Core Principle

Use:

> Emerging means scientifically forming, not simply fashionable.

Do not equate:

* high publication volume;
* media attention;
* keyword popularity;
* citation count;
* technological hype;

with genuine scientific emergence.

---

# Activation Conditions

Use this skill when the researcher asks:

* "What topics are emerging?"
* "What new research areas are developing?"
* "What is the next frontier in this field?"
* "Which topics are still early but growing?"
* "What new methodologies are appearing?"
* "Where are disciplines beginning to converge?"
* "What research opportunities may become important in the next few years?"
* "Which new research directions should I investigate?"

Prefer upstream context from:

`research-landscape`
→ `trend-detection`

---

# Relationship with Trend Detection

`trend-detection` answers:

> What is changing?

`emerging-topic-discovery` answers:

> Which changes represent the formation of a genuinely new scientific direction?

A topic may be:

`STRONG_TREND`

but not:

`EMERGING_TOPIC`

because it may already be mature.

Likewise, an emerging topic may still have:

* relatively few publications;
* limited citations;
* limited methodological standardization.

---

# Evidence Requirement

Reliable emerging-topic detection usually requires:

* recent literature;
* historical comparison;
* terminology evolution;
* research-theme comparison;
* methodological development;
* evidence maturity assessment.

Preferred evidence route:

`scopus-literature-search`
→ `source-verification`
→ `literature-screening`
→ `research-landscape`
→ `trend-detection`
→ `emerging-topic-discovery`

When comprehensive evidence is unavailable, label findings:

`PRELIMINARY_EMERGING_SIGNAL`

---

# Scopus-First Policy

When external evidence is required, prioritize:

1. peer-reviewed articles from active Scopus-indexed journals;
2. recent high-quality primary studies;
3. recent systematic reviews when available;
4. methodological papers introducing important new approaches;
5. seminal literature needed to understand the field's previous baseline;
6. contradictory or cautionary evidence.

Other scholarly databases may support discovery and verification.

Never claim Scopus indexing without verification.

---

# 1. Define Emerging Relative to a Baseline

Emergence can only be judged relative to what existed previously.

Determine:

* historical concepts;
* established themes;
* dominant methods;
* previous technologies;
* previous research questions.

Then identify what appears meaningfully new.

Without a baseline, novelty of emergence cannot be judged.

---

# 2. Appropriate Time Horizon

Use a time window appropriate to field velocity.

Possible windows:

* last 2–3 years for very fast-moving fields;
* last 5 years for moderately dynamic fields;
* longer windows for slower fields.

Do not impose one fixed window across all disciplines.

---

# 3. New-Term Signal

Identify terminology that appears recently or increases rapidly.

Possible signals:

* newly coined concept;
* newly standardized terminology;
* new methodological term;
* new technology label.

Use:

`NEW_TERM_SIGNAL`

but do not conclude emergence based only on terminology.

A renamed established concept is not necessarily new science.

---

# 4. Conceptual Emergence

Identify genuinely new conceptual structures.

Possible forms:

* new construct;
* new mechanism;
* new conceptual relationship;
* integration of previously separate concepts.

Classify:

* `NEW_CONCEPT_SIGNAL`
* `CONCEPT_REFRAMING`
* `CONCEPT_INTEGRATION`
* `TERMINOLOGY_CHANGE_ONLY`

Distinguish conceptual innovation from relabeling.

---

# 5. Research Question Emergence

Identify newly appearing question types.

Example:

A mature field may historically ask:

> Is X associated with Y?

Recent studies may begin asking:

> Can X predict Y prospectively?

or:

> Can this mechanism be modified through intervention?

This may represent:

`NEW_RESEARCH_QUESTION_FRONTIER`

---

# 6. Methodological Emergence

Identify recently introduced or rapidly adopted methods.

Examples:

* new assay;
* new imaging technique;
* causal inference approach;
* digital phenotyping;
* machine-learning architecture;
* novel qualitative integration;
* new measurement technique.

Classify:

* `NEW_METHOD`
* `EARLY_METHOD_ADOPTION`
* `METHOD_EXPANSION`
* `MATURE_METHOD`

A method is not a research topic by itself unless it enables new scientific questions.

---

# 7. Technology-Enabled Emergence

A technology may create new research possibilities.

Examples:

* AI;
* omics;
* biosensors;
* wearable technologies;
* digital twins;
* advanced biomaterials;
* remote sensing;
* immersive learning;
* automated laboratory systems.

Ask:

> What previously unanswerable scientific question can this technology now address?

Do not recommend research merely because a technology is popular.

---

# 8. Data-Enabled Emergence

New data sources may create emerging research areas.

Examples:

* electronic health records;
* large-scale genomic datasets;
* sensor streams;
* learning analytics;
* satellite data;
* administrative longitudinal data.

Identify whether new data enable:

* larger samples;
* longitudinal analysis;
* real-world evidence;
* multimodal integration;
* improved prediction.

---

# 9. Interdisciplinary Emergence

Identify convergence between previously separate research areas.

Examples:

```text
pharmacy + pharmacogenomics
education + artificial intelligence
ecology + machine learning
psychology + digital health
materials science + wound healing
```

Classify:

* `EARLY_INTERDISCIPLINARY_CONVERGENCE`
* `ESTABLISHED_INTERDISCIPLINARY_FIELD`
* `SPECULATIVE_COMBINATION`

Do not treat arbitrary discipline combinations as emergence.

---

# 10. Mechanism Emergence

A field may shift from association toward explanation.

Signals include increasing research on:

* pathways;
* mediators;
* molecular mechanisms;
* behavioral processes;
* social mechanisms;
* organizational mechanisms.

This may indicate:

`MECHANISTIC_FRONTIER`

---

# 11. Validation Emergence

A field may begin transitioning from development to validation.

Look for increases in:

* independent validation;
* multicenter validation;
* external datasets;
* replication;
* cross-cultural validation.

This may represent:

`VALIDATION_FRONTIER`

rather than a completely new topic.

---

# 12. Prediction Emergence

A field may move from explanation toward prediction.

Signals include:

* prognostic modeling;
* classification;
* risk stratification;
* forecasting;
* individualized prediction;
* predictive validation.

Classify:

`PREDICTION_FRONTIER`

when supported.

---

# 13. Intervention Emergence

An established observational field may begin testing interventions.

Look for:

* pilot interventions;
* controlled trials;
* feasibility studies;
* intervention-development frameworks.

This may represent:

`INTERVENTION_FRONTIER`

---

# 14. Implementation Emergence

Mature interventions may generate new work on:

* feasibility;
* adoption;
* fidelity;
* scalability;
* sustainability;
* implementation barriers.

Classify:

`IMPLEMENTATION_FRONTIER`

---

# 15. Translational Emergence

Basic evidence may begin moving toward:

* clinical application;
* educational practice;
* policy;
* product development;
* industrial application;
* community implementation.

Use:

`TRANSLATIONAL_FRONTIER`

when several signals support the transition.

---

# 16. Multi-Signal Emergence Rule

Do not classify a topic as strongly emerging from a single signal.

Stronger evidence occurs when several indicators converge.

For example:

```text
new terminology
+
rapid recent publication growth
+
new methodological adoption
+
cross-disciplinary diffusion
+
recent high-quality studies
+
unresolved questions
```

This provides stronger evidence than publication growth alone.

---

# 17. Emergence Signal Types

Possible signals include:

* new terminology;
* rapid recent growth;
* increasing topic share;
* new methods;
* new technology;
* new data;
* cross-disciplinary convergence;
* newly appearing reviews;
* new research-question types;
* early replication;
* rapidly growing citation attention;
* increasing international diffusion.

No single signal is sufficient in all cases.

---

# 18. Emergence Confidence

Use:

* `STRONG_EMERGING_TOPIC`
* `MODERATE_EMERGING_TOPIC`
* `EARLY_EMERGING_SIGNAL`
* `SPECULATIVE_SIGNAL`
* `NOT_EMERGING`

Explain the evidence supporting the classification.

---

# 19. Emerging vs Established

Classify candidates conceptually as:

## Emerging

Newly forming and still scientifically developing.

## Growing Established

Well-established area with continued expansion.

## Mature Stable

Scientifically established and relatively stable.

## Declining

Research attention or relevance appears to be decreasing.

## Reframed

An older topic has been reorganized under new concepts.

## Uncertain

Evidence insufficient.

This distinction is essential.

---

# 20. Emerging Does Not Mean Novel Study

Even a genuinely emerging field may already contain many similar studies.

Therefore:

`EMERGING_TOPIC`

does not equal:

`VALIDATED_NOVELTY`

Novelty must later be assessed through:

`sota-builder`
→ `gap-discovery`
→ `gap-validator`
→ `novelty-builder`
→ `novelty-auditor`

---

# 21. Emerging Does Not Mean Strong Evidence

Early fields often have:

* small studies;
* exploratory methods;
* limited replication;
* inconsistent terminology;
* uncertain measurement.

Therefore assess:

`EVIDENCE_MATURITY`

separately from:

`EMERGENCE_STRENGTH`

---

# 22. Evidence Maturity Classification

Use when useful:

* `VERY_EARLY`
* `EARLY`
* `DEVELOPING`
* `MODERATELY_MATURE`
* `MATURE`

An emerging topic is often:

`VERY_EARLY`
or:
`EARLY`

but not always.

---

# 23. Emerging Topic Saturation

Some new topics become crowded rapidly.

Assess:

* `LOW_SATURATION`
* `MODERATE_SATURATION`
* `HIGH_SATURATION`
* `UNKNOWN`

A low-saturation emerging topic may offer opportunity but also higher uncertainty.

A high-saturation emerging topic may require stronger differentiation.

---

# 24. Durability Signal

Assess whether emergence may represent a durable scientific direction.

Possible classifications:

* `LIKELY_DURABLE`
* `POSSIBLY_DURABLE`
* `EARLY_UNCERTAIN`
* `POSSIBLY_TRANSIENT`

Consider:

* underlying scientific problem;
* methodological utility;
* theoretical relevance;
* replication;
* real-world need;
* translational potential.

Do not forecast confidently.

---

# 25. Hype Risk

Identify topics driven heavily by fashion or technology marketing.

Use:

* `LOW_HYPE_RISK`
* `MODERATE_HYPE_RISK`
* `HIGH_HYPE_RISK`
* `UNKNOWN`

Possible hype signals:

* rapid publication increase without strong evidence;
* repeated conceptual papers with little empirical validation;
* technology-first research with unclear scientific questions;
* exaggerated claims;
* unstable terminology.

Do not dismiss a field merely because it attracts attention.

---

# 26. Scientific Problem Persistence

A durable emerging field usually addresses a persistent scientific or practical problem.

Ask:

> If the fashionable technology or terminology disappeared, would the underlying scientific problem still matter?

If yes, durability may be stronger.

---

# 27. Early Contradictions

Emerging topics often contain contradictory evidence.

Record:

`EARLY_CONTRADICTION_SIGNAL`

This may create valuable research opportunities later.

Do not prematurely resolve contradictory findings.

---

# 28. Measurement Instability

Emerging fields may lack standardized measurement.

Possible signals:

* multiple competing scales;
* changing definitions;
* inconsistent operationalization;
* unvalidated instruments.

Record:

`MEASUREMENT_FRONTIER_SIGNAL`

This may later generate methodological research opportunities.

---

# 29. Terminology Instability

Different authors may use different names for similar phenomena.

Record:

`TERMINOLOGY_INSTABILITY`

and pass synonyms downstream to literature-search workflows.

Failure to handle terminology may produce false research gaps.

---

# 30. Standardization Signal

A field may begin converging toward:

* common definitions;
* standard measures;
* reporting guidelines;
* shared protocols.

This may signal transition from:

early emergence

toward:

field consolidation.

---

# 31. Review-Literature Signal

The appearance of:

* systematic reviews;
* scoping reviews;
* meta-analyses;

may indicate that enough primary evidence has accumulated for synthesis.

However, review publication alone does not mean the field is mature.

Assess what the reviews conclude.

---

# 32. Replication Signal

The appearance of independent replication may indicate maturation.

Distinguish:

* discovery;
* replication;
* validation;
* implementation.

This helps identify where the scientific frontier has moved.

---

# 33. International Diffusion

Emerging research may spread from a few institutions or countries into broader networks.

Classify:

* `LOCALIZED_EMERGENCE`
* `REGIONAL_DIFFUSION`
* `GLOBAL_DIFFUSION`

Do not interpret geographic spread as evidence quality.

---

# 34. Institutional Diffusion

When bibliographic metadata support it, identify whether an area is moving from:

* one research group;
* several groups;
* many independent institutions.

Independent adoption may strengthen emergence evidence.

---

# 35. Journal Diffusion

Determine whether a topic appears across:

* one specialty journal;
* several specialty journals;
* broader disciplinary journals;
* multidisciplinary journals.

Broad diffusion may indicate growing relevance.

Do not infer quality solely from journal count.

---

# 36. Target-Journal Signal

If the researcher has a target journal, determine whether relevant emerging work appears there.

Use this only as one contextual signal.

Do not overrepresent target-journal publications.

Target-journal citation must remain scientifically justified.

# 39. Strong Candidate Criteria

A strong emerging-topic candidate generally shows several of:

* meaningful scientific problem;
* recent conceptual or methodological development;
* increasing but not purely hype-driven attention;
* evidence of independent research-group adoption;
* unresolved scientific questions;
* feasible research pathway;
* potential long-term relevance.

No candidate must satisfy every criterion.

---

# 40. Weak Candidate Warning

Be cautious when a topic appears attractive mainly because:

* it is fashionable;
* it contains AI;
* publication volume is high;
* few papers exist;
* it has not been studied locally;
* the terminology sounds new.

These are insufficient alone.

---

# 41. Emerging Opportunity vs Research Gap

Use:

`EMERGING_RESEARCH_OPPORTUNITY_SIGNAL`

not:

`CONFIRMED_GAP`

until evidence synthesis and gap validation occur.

Required downstream logic:

```text
Emerging Topic
      ↓
Scopus-first Evidence
      ↓
State of the Art
      ↓
Gap Discovery
      ↓
Gap Validation
      ↓
Novelty
```

---

# 42. Scopus-First Deepening

When a candidate emerging topic is selected for deeper investigation, prioritize:

* active Scopus-indexed journals;
* recent relevant primary articles;
* systematic reviews when available;
* methodological source papers;
* contradictory evidence;
* adjacent terminology.

Do not rely on search-engine snippets as final evidence.

---

# 43. Source Quality

A rapidly emerging topic may contain lower-quality early evidence.

Do not lower source-quality standards simply because literature is sparse.

Use:

`source-verification`

to distinguish:

* peer-reviewed evidence;
* preprints;
* conference abstracts;
* editorials;
* conceptual papers.

---

# 44. Preprints

Preprints may be useful in fast-moving emerging fields.

If used:

* clearly identify them as preprints;
* do not treat them as peer-reviewed evidence;
* verify whether a peer-reviewed version later appeared.

Prioritize peer-reviewed evidence when available.

---

# 45. No-Mandatory-APC Policy

APC status does not affect whether a scientific topic is emerging.

Do not exclude literature because of journal publication cost.

Preserve:

`prefer_no_mandatory_apc`

for later:

`journal-matcher`
and:
`no-apc-journal-finder`

---

# 46. Publication Potential

An emerging topic may have publication potential, but do not promise acceptance.

Assess only provisional:

* international relevance;
* scientific importance;
* field development;
* potential contribution.

Final journal strategy belongs downstream.

---

# 47. Research Program Potential

Emerging topics may support long-term research programs.

Possible progression:

```text
Discovery
      ↓
Characterization
      ↓
Mechanism
      ↓
Validation
      ↓
Intervention
      ↓
Implementation
```

Use:

* `HIGH_PROGRAM_POTENTIAL`
* `MODERATE_PROGRAM_POTENTIAL`
* `LIMITED_PROGRAM_POTENTIAL`
* `UNKNOWN`

Do not construct the final roadmap here.

---

# 48. Relationship with Idea Discovery

`idea-discovery` generates possible research directions.

`emerging-topic-discovery` assesses whether some directions represent newly developing scientific frontiers.

They are not substitutes.

---

# 49. Relationship with Research Landscape

`research-landscape` provides structural context.

Without landscape context, apparent emergence may simply reflect incomplete knowledge of the field.

---

# 50. Relationship with Trend Detection

`trend-detection` identifies temporal change.

`emerging-topic-discovery` evaluates whether that change reflects new scientific formation.

---

# 51. Relationship with State of the Art

Emerging-topic analysis does not establish the State of the Art.

`sota-builder` later determines:

* established knowledge;
* current best evidence;
* contested knowledge;
* emerging evidence;
* unresolved problems.

---

# 52. Relationship with Gap Discovery

An emerging area may produce gap signals.

But:

`gap-discovery`

and:

`gap-validator`

must determine whether a genuine research gap exists.

---

# 53. Relationship with Novelty

Emergence is contextual evidence for novelty, not novelty itself.

Do not write:

> "The topic is emerging, therefore the proposed study is novel."

Novelty requires comparison with specific existing work.

# 56. User-Friendly Behavior

Explain the difference between trend and emergence clearly.

Prefer:

> AI-supported assessment is growing rapidly, but it is already a well-established research stream. In contrast, multimodal AI assessment using behavioral and physiological data appears much earlier in development and may represent an emerging frontier. We still need to check the current literature before considering it a genuine research gap.

Avoid:

> AI is trending, therefore it is novel.

---

# 57. Avoid These Behaviors

Do not:

* equate trend with emergence;
* equate emergence with novelty;
* equate sparse literature with opportunity;
* equate new terminology with new science;
* chase fashionable technologies;
* fabricate publication-growth statistics;
* fabricate citation data;
* infer the entire field from one journal;
* ignore contradictory evidence;
* ignore evidence quality;
* treat target-journal publications as representative of the entire field;
* allow APC policy to bias evidence selection.

---

# Stop Conditions

Do not classify a topic as strongly emerging when:

* historical baseline is absent;
* terminology is unstable and unresolved;
* temporal evidence is inadequate;
* only a handful of unverified sources exist;
* apparent emergence could be a renamed established concept;
* evidence comes from one research group only;
* publication growth is the only signal.

Use a provisional classification and identify what must be checked next.

---

# Success Criterion

`emerging-topic-discovery` succeeds when newly forming scientific directions have been distinguished from mature trends, fashionable terminology, and publication growth using multiple evidence signals, and the strongest candidates are ready for rigorous Scopus-first evidence investigation without prematurely claiming research gaps or novelty.
