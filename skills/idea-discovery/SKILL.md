---

name: idea-discovery
description: Help researchers transform broad interests, real-world problems, disciplinary expertise, emerging phenomena, available resources, or unresolved scientific questions into a focused set of promising research directions. Use when a researcher wants a new research idea, has only a broad topic, wants to open a new research line, or needs research directions that can later be evaluated through current Scopus-first evidence, gap validation, novelty assessment, feasibility, and publication potential.
---

# Idea Discovery

## Purpose

`idea-discovery` helps researchers move from:

* broad interest;
* practical problem;
* disciplinary curiosity;
* emerging phenomenon;
* new technology;
* observed anomaly;
* policy challenge;
* clinical problem;
* educational problem;
* social problem;
* environmental problem;

toward a small number of scientifically meaningful research directions.

Its purpose is **not** to immediately generate polished research titles.

Its purpose is to identify:

> What scientific questions are worth investigating next?

All ideas generated at this stage are provisional until they are evaluated against current literature.

---

# Core Principle

Use:

> Problem first. Question second. Evidence third. Gap fourth. Novelty later.

Do not begin with:

> "Let's invent a unique title."

A research idea should emerge from a meaningful scientific or practical uncertainty.

---

# Activation Conditions

Use this skill when the researcher says or implies:

* "I want a new research idea."
* "I do not know what to study."
* "What research can I do in this field?"
* "I am interested in X."
* "What is an interesting topic in this area?"
* "I want to start a new research line."
* "What new research could be developed from this problem?"
* "What research opportunities exist around this technology?"
* "I want a topic suitable for international publication."

Prefer upstream context from:

`research-intake`

when available.

---

# Idea Discovery Is Not Gap Validation

This skill may generate:

`RESEARCH_DIRECTION`

or:

`PROVISIONAL_RESEARCH_OPPORTUNITY`

It must not claim:

* "Nobody has studied this."
* "This is a research gap."
* "This is novel."
* "This will publish in Q1."
* "This topic is Scopus-worthy."

Those claims require downstream evidence.

# 1. Clarify the Broad Interest

Identify what the researcher actually wants to understand.

For example:

Broad:

"Chitosan"

Refine conceptually into possibilities such as:

* extraction efficiency;
* physicochemical properties;
* biological activity;
* formulation;
* drug delivery;
* wound healing;
* antimicrobial mechanism;
* industrial application.

Do not immediately choose among them.

---

# 2. Identify the Underlying Problem

Ask:

> What makes this topic scientifically or practically important?

Classify the starting problem where possible.

Possible types:

* `THEORETICAL_PROBLEM`
* `EMPIRICAL_INCONSISTENCY`
* `MECHANISTIC_UNCERTAINTY`
* `MEASUREMENT_PROBLEM`
* `METHODOLOGICAL_PROBLEM`
* `CLINICAL_PROBLEM`
* `EDUCATIONAL_PROBLEM`
* `TECHNOLOGICAL_PROBLEM`
* `ENVIRONMENTAL_PROBLEM`
* `SOCIAL_PROBLEM`
* `ORGANIZATIONAL_PROBLEM`
* `POLICY_PROBLEM`
* `IMPLEMENTATION_PROBLEM`
* `TRANSLATIONAL_PROBLEM`
* `UNKNOWN`

---

# 3. Distinguish Topic from Research Problem

A topic is not yet a research problem.

Example:

Topic:

> Artificial intelligence in higher education.

Potential scientific problems:

* uncertainty about learning effectiveness;
* bias in assessment;
* adoption barriers;
* changes in academic integrity;
* effectiveness of AI-supported feedback;
* unequal access.

Do not treat a keyword as a research question.

---

# 4. Identify the Knowledge Objective

Determine what kind of knowledge may be needed.

Possible goals:

* discover;
* characterize;
* describe;
* compare;
* explain;
* test;
* validate;
* predict;
* develop;
* optimize;
* intervene;
* implement;
* translate;
* synthesize.

Do not choose a method yet.

---

# 5. Research Direction Generation

Generate a focused set of possible research directions.

Default:

3–7 directions.

Each direction should be scientifically distinct.

Avoid generating ten variations of the same idea.

Possible direction types include:

* descriptive;
* mechanistic;
* comparative;
* predictive;
* methodological;
* longitudinal;
* intervention;
* implementation;
* translational;
* integrative;
* validation;
* technology-enabled.

# 7. Scientific Importance

Estimate whether the direction addresses a meaningful issue.

Use:

* `HIGH_POTENTIAL`
* `MODERATE_POTENTIAL`
* `LOW_POTENTIAL`
* `NOT_ASSESSABLE`

This is preliminary.

Scientific importance must later be supported by literature and context.

---

# 8. Researchability

A promising research direction should be investigable.

Consider:

* clear phenomenon;
* observable or measurable outcome;
* accessible population/data/material;
* researchable question;
* feasible evidence collection.

Classify:

* `HIGHLY_RESEARCHABLE`
* `RESEARCHABLE_WITH_REFINEMENT`
* `DIFFICULT_BUT_POSSIBLE`
* `CURRENTLY_UNCLEAR`

Do not discard ambitious ideas too early.

---

# 9. Scope Control

Avoid research directions that are too broad.

Weak:

> Investigate the impact of AI on education.

Better:

> Examine how AI-supported formative feedback affects specific learning outcomes under defined instructional conditions.

Do not finalize wording before literature evaluation.

---

# 10. Avoid Variable Shopping

Do not generate ideas by randomly combining variables.

Bad pattern:

> X + Y + mediator Z + moderator M because no one has tested them together.

A valid conceptual combination requires:

* theoretical rationale;
* empirical signal;
* mechanistic logic;
* practical relevance.

More variables do not equal more novelty.

---

# 11. Avoid Geographic Novelty

Do not recommend:

> "Study X in City Y because it has never been studied there."

unless the context tests a scientifically meaningful difference.

Context may matter when it changes:

* mechanism;
* institutional environment;
* cultural conditions;
* exposure;
* implementation;
* population characteristics;
* external validity.

Geography alone is weak novelty.

---

# 12. Avoid Method-as-Novelty

Do not assume:

* SEM;
* PLS-SEM;
* machine learning;
* AI;
* mixed methods;

automatically create novelty.

Methods are justified by research questions.

A method may contribute novelty only when it solves a genuine methodological limitation.

---

# 13. Avoid Trend Chasing

A popular topic is not automatically a strong research opportunity.

Trend signals must later be evaluated for:

* scientific importance;
* evidence maturity;
* unresolved questions;
* feasibility;
* durability.

Do not recommend research solely because publication volume is increasing.

---

# 14. Problem-Driven Idea Sources

Strong ideas may originate from:

## Contradictions

Different studies report inconsistent results.

## Boundary Conditions

An effect may differ by context or population.

## Mechanism Uncertainty

A relationship is known but why it occurs remains unclear.

## Validation Need

A model or instrument lacks independent validation.

## Temporal Uncertainty

Cross-sectional evidence cannot explain change over time.

## Implementation Failure

An effective intervention does not work in real-world settings.

## Translation Gap

Basic findings have not reached practice.

## Measurement Limitation

Existing instruments inadequately capture the phenomenon.

## Emerging Technology

A new capability creates previously impossible research questions.

## Integration Opportunity

Two previously separate research areas may solve a common problem.

These are only candidate signals until literature confirms them.

---

# 15. Research Maturity Awareness

Consider the maturity of the field.

Possible stages:

* discovery;
* characterization;
* association;
* mechanism;
* validation;
* prediction;
* intervention;
* implementation;
* translation.

A strong idea often advances the field to its next appropriate maturity stage.

Do not recommend intervention when fundamental evidence is still weak.

---

# 16. Researcher Capability Awareness

When upstream intake provides researcher capabilities, use them.

Possible resources:

* laboratory;
* clinical population;
* educational institution;
* existing dataset;
* software expertise;
* molecular equipment;
* qualitative field access;
* industry collaboration.

These may help identify feasible directions.

Do not restrict ideas only to current capability.

Mark when:

`COLLABORATION_REQUIRED`

or:

`NEW_CAPABILITY_REQUIRED`

---

# 17. Interdisciplinary Opportunities

Consider interdisciplinary research when it solves a genuine scientific problem.

Examples:

* pharmacy + AI;
* education + learning analytics;
* biomedical science + data science;
* environment + economics;
* psychology + digital technology.

Do not combine disciplines merely for novelty.

Interdisciplinary research should create explanatory or methodological value.

---

# 18. Translational Potential

When relevant, identify whether a research direction could eventually move toward:

* practice;
* policy;
* intervention;
* clinical decision;
* technology;
* product;
* implementation.

Use:

`TRANSLATIONAL_POTENTIAL`

as a planning signal, not a promise.

---

# 19. Long-Term Research Program Potential

Some ideas are stronger because they can support multiple studies.

For example:

```text
Discovery
   ↓
Mechanism
   ↓
Validation
   ↓
Intervention
   ↓
Implementation
```

Classify:

* `HIGH_PROGRAM_POTENTIAL`
* `MODERATE_PROGRAM_POTENTIAL`
* `LIMITED_PROGRAM_POTENTIAL`
* `UNKNOWN`

Do not create the final roadmap here.

---

# 20. Evidence Requirement

Every promising direction must eventually be evaluated against current evidence.

The typical next route is:

`research-landscape`

followed by evidence-search workflows.

Do not finalize a direction as a research opportunity before this occurs.

---

# 21. Scopus-First Evidence Policy

When literature investigation begins, prioritize:

1. peer-reviewed articles from active Scopus-indexed journals;
2. high-quality systematic reviews and meta-analyses when relevant;
3. recent high-quality primary studies;
4. seminal foundational literature;
5. contradictory evidence;
6. emerging methods.

Other scholarly platforms may support discovery and verification.

Never claim Scopus indexing without verification.

---

# 22. Publication-Aware Idea Discovery

If the researcher aims for international publication, consider whether a direction is:

* internationally relevant;
* scientifically consequential;
* capable of generating a defensible contribution;
* methodologically appropriate.

Do not optimize ideas solely for publication.

Scientific validity comes first.

---

# 23. No-Mandatory-APC Preference

If the Research Passport contains:

`prefer_no_mandatory_apc: true`

preserve this preference for downstream journal selection.

Do not let journal cost determine the research question.

The later publication workflow should seek:

* strong scientific fit;
* active Scopus indexing;
* credible journal quality;
* no mandatory APC where comparable options exist.

---

# 24. Target-Journal Awareness

If the researcher already has a target journal, record it.

Do not shape the research idea artificially around the target journal.

Later:

`target-journal-intelligence`

may analyze relevant publications from that journal to determine legitimate scholarly fit.

---

# 25. Preliminary Idea Ranking

When several directions exist, compare provisionally using:

* scientific importance;
* researchability;
* evidence need;
* feasibility;
* long-term program potential;
* interdisciplinary potential;
* translational potential.

Do not include novelty as confirmed.

Use:

`NOVELTY_REQUIRES_VALIDATION`

# 27. Candidate Status

Use:

* `PROMISING_DIRECTION`
* `PROMISING_WITH_REFINEMENT`
* `REQUIRES_LANDSCAPE_CHECK`
* `HIGH_FEASIBILITY`
* `HIGH_RISK_HIGH_POTENTIAL`
* `LOW_PRIORITY`
* `INSUFFICIENT_INFORMATION`

Avoid exaggerated rankings.

---

# 28. Recommended Directions

When enough context exists, identify:

## Primary Direction

The most promising starting point.

## Alternative Direction A

A scientifically meaningful alternative.

## Alternative Direction B

Another defensible option.

Explain differences.

Do not pretend one direction is definitively superior before evidence mapping.

---

# 29. User Decision Support

When researchers must choose among directions, explain trade-offs.

Example:

> The mechanistic direction offers stronger scientific progression but requires laboratory collaboration. The validation direction is easier to execute and could establish external validity, but its contribution may be more incremental.

Decision support is more useful than a simple ranking.

---

# 30. Preliminary Research Question

For promising directions, a provisional research question may be provided.

Label:

`PROVISIONAL_RESEARCH_QUESTION`

Do not finalize it until literature mapping clarifies:

* terminology;
* established evidence;
* unresolved questions;
* appropriate scope.

Final RQ development belongs to:

`research-question-builder`

---

# 31. Do Not Generate Final Titles Too Early

A title may lock the researcher into unsupported assumptions.

If the user explicitly asks for title examples, provide them as:

`PROVISIONAL_TITLE`

and explain that they may change after literature and gap validation.

---

# 32. Relationship with Research Landscape

`idea-discovery` identifies candidate directions.

`research-landscape` determines:

> What does the field currently look like around those directions?

Typical sequence:

```text
Broad Interest
      ↓
idea-discovery
      ↓
Candidate Directions
      ↓
research-landscape
```

---

# 33. Relationship with Trend Detection

`idea-discovery` should not independently conclude that a topic is trending.

Trend evaluation belongs to:

`trend-detection`

---

# 34. Relationship with Emerging Topic Discovery

A research direction may appear new.

However:

`emerging-topic-discovery`

should later determine whether the topic is genuinely emerging based on current literature signals.

---

# 35. Relationship with Gap Discovery

An interesting direction is not automatically a research gap.

Later:

`gap-discovery`
→ `gap-validator`

must establish whether an unresolved opportunity exists.

---

# 36. Relationship with Novelty Builder

# 37. Relationship with Continuation Opportunity Finder

`idea-discovery` primarily handles new research directions.

`continuation-opportunity-finder` handles next-study choices grounded in previous research.

If previous research already exists and should be continued, prefer:

`continuation-opportunity-finder`

rather than restarting with broad idea discovery.

# 40. Avoid These Behaviors

Do not:

* generate random topic lists;
* equate keywords with research questions;
* claim novelty without literature;
* claim research gaps without validation;
* manufacture complex models;
* add arbitrary mediators or moderators;
* equate a new location with novelty;
* equate advanced statistics with novelty;
* chase trends without scientific purpose;
* optimize research purely for journal acceptance;
* guarantee Scopus publication;
* fabricate citations;
* create unsupported research titles.

---

# 41. User-Friendly Behavior

For novice researchers:

present a few clear directions and explain why each matters.

For experienced researchers:

focus on scientific uncertainties, field maturity, program potential, and evidence requirements.

Do not overwhelm users with the internal framework.

Instead of saying:

> "We now invoke research-landscape."

Prefer:

> "These directions are promising, but before selecting one we should map the current literature to see which questions are already well answered and which remain genuinely open."

---

# Stop Conditions

Do not recommend a confident research direction when:

* the user's interest is too vague to identify a meaningful problem;
* the research goal is unclear;
* the direction depends on unsupported assumptions;
* feasibility hinges on unknown critical resources.

Ask only the minimum clarification needed.

---

# Success Criterion

`idea-discovery` succeeds when a broad interest or real-world problem has been transformed into a small set of scientifically meaningful, researchable, and strategically useful candidate directions that are ready for current literature landscape mapping without prematurely claiming research gaps or novelty.
